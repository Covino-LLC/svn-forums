## PLANT 11 — Biosphere
### Who Pays for Access: How Private Money Moves Through Federal Elections and Lobbying

Private money reaches federal politics through four channels, and each has its own rules. Contributions to candidates are capped and disclosed. Super PACs have no cap and must disclose donors, but they may not coordinate with campaigns. Some nonprofits, mainly 501(c)(4)s, can spend on elections without naming their donors. Lobbying is disclosed spending to influence policy between elections. Concern about this crosses party lines. In Pew's survey of April 20–26, 2026, 74% of Americans called the role of money in politics a "very big problem": 79% of Democrats and Democratic leaners, and 70% of Republicans and Republican leaners. The structural question is not which side benefits. It is whether these channels give a small number of funders measurably more access or policy influence than other citizens.

**How the uncapped channels opened:** *Citizens United v. FEC* (2010) struck down limits on independent spending by corporations. Later in 2010, *SpeechNow.org v. FEC* applied the same reasoning to groups that do not give directly to candidates, which let them accept unlimited contributions. These groups became super PACs. Super PACs spent about $6.4 billion on federal elections from 2010 to 2022, and a record of at least $2.7 billion in 2024. Spending by nonprofits that do not disclose donors rose from less than $5 million in 2006 to more than $1 billion in 2024.

**Concentration at the top:** In the 2022 midterms, 21 donor families gave $783 million, and billionaires supplied 15% of all federal election financing (Brennan Center analysis).

**Lobbying between elections:** Reported federal lobbying reached a record $4.4 billion in 2024, up $150 million from 2023 (OpenSecrets data). The National Association of Realtors spent the most ($86 million), followed by the U.S. Chamber of Commerce ($76.2 million). By sector, pharmaceuticals spent the most, at $384 million.

**What the evidence shows on influence:** Gilens and Page looked at 1,779 proposed policy changes from 1981 to 2002. They found that the preferences of affluent Americans predicted which policies were adopted, while the preferences of middle-income Americans did not. Enns replied that the two groups' preferences differed by 10 points or less on 82% of those policies, and that their rankings of policies correlated at r = 0.94. He called this "coincidental representation": the middle often gets what it wants because the affluent want the same thing. Access is easier to measure than influence. In a randomized experiment across 191 congressional offices, groups identified as donors got meetings with the member or chief of staff 12.5% of the time, compared with 2.4% for groups identified only as constituents. That shows a gap in access. It does not show that votes changed.

**Proposed solution:** Seattle's Democracy Voucher program, running since 2017, gives every registered voter four $25 vouchers to give to city candidates. Griffith and Noonen (2022) compared Seattle with similar cities and found a 350% rise in unique donors, a 270% rise in small donations and 86% more candidates per race. Incumbents became less likely to be re-elected. Yorgason (2025), studying the same program, found that voucher users came mostly from groups that were already overrepresented among donors. Participation rose 0.21 points with each income decile, white residents were 2.5% more likely to take part, and Republicans were 3.25% less likely. The trade-offs: the program is paid for with public money, and it does not reach super PAC or nonprofit spending, which court rulings protect.

**The testable question:** In cities that adopt vouchers or small-donor matching, does the share of candidate money from donors giving $200 or less rise within two election cycles? And does the donor pool's income and demographic profile move closer to that of registered voters, compared with similar cities, as measured in public campaign filings?

## PLANT 12 — Biosphere
### Twice the Spending, Shorter Lives: How Prices, Administration and Coverage Rules Decide Who Pays for U.S. Health Care

In a Pew survey fielded April 20–26, 2026, 73% of adults called health care affordability a "very big problem": 85% of Democrats and 60% of Republicans. In Gallup's March 2–18, 2026 poll, 61% said they worry "a great deal" about it, the highest of 16 domestic issues. The mechanism behind this has three layers. The first is what each service costs. The second is the middle layers between the patient and the provider: insurers, pharmacy benefit managers (PBMs) and billing. The third is the set of subsidy and eligibility rules that decide how much of the bill reaches households directly.

**Spending vs. outcomes:** In 2024 the U.S. spent $14,775 per person on health care. The average across comparable wealthy countries was $7,860, and the U.S. figure was almost $5,000 above Switzerland, the next-highest. Health care took 17.2% of U.S. GDP against an 11.2% peer average. U.S. life expectancy reached 79.0 years in 2024, its highest ever, but the peer average was 82.7.

**Where the money goes:** In 2022, employers and private insurers paid hospitals 254% of what Medicare would have paid for the same services, and 289% for outpatient services. That ratio varies by state for identical services. It was below 200% in Arkansas, Iowa, Massachusetts, Michigan and Mississippi, and above 300% in California, Florida, Georgia, New York, South Carolina, West Virginia and Wisconsin. Administrative costs are estimated at 15–30% of medical spending. About half of that is classed as wasteful, which comes to $285–570 billion a year (2019 figures). U.S. administrative spending is $1,055 per person, against $306 in Germany. U.S. prescription drug prices averaged 2.78 times those in 33 other countries in 2022. Brand-name drugs were 4.22 times as expensive, while generics cost about 67% of the foreign price. The three largest PBMs processed nearly 80% of about 6.6 billion U.S. prescriptions in 2023, and they are owned together with the largest insurers and pharmacies.

**Who absorbs it:** About 20 million adults, roughly 1 in 12, owed medical debt, totaling at least $220 billion (2021 data). The enhanced ACA premium tax credits expired at the end of 2025, and the 2025 budget reconciliation law did not extend them. Between 2025 and 2026, the average net marketplace premium rose from $113 to $178 a month (+58%). The average deductible rose from $2,759 to $3,786. Enrollment is projected at 17.5 million, down from 22.3 million. The same law requires adults aged 19–64 in the Medicaid expansion group to work or do qualifying activities for 80 hours a month, starting no later than December 31, 2026. CBO estimates this saves $326 billion in federal spending over 10 years and adds 5.3 million uninsured people by 2034. Supporters point to the federal savings. Critics point to the coverage losses. CBO estimates both.

**Proposed solution:** All-payer hospital budgets, which Maryland has run under its Total Cost of Care Model. Each hospital receives a fixed annual revenue amount, and all insurers pay rates set by the state. Mathematica's evaluation found that in 2019–2021 the model cut Medicare fee-for-service spending by 2.5% ($781 million), cut acute admissions by 16.1% and cut unplanned readmissions by 9.5%, while improving several quality measures. The trade-offs: spending outside hospitals rose sharply in 2021, which reduced that year's savings; the evaluation did not measure effects on commercial insurers; and hospitals take on the financial risk if patient volume changes. Maryland moves to CMS's AHEAD model in 2026.

**The testable question:** From 2026 to 2030, does per-person hospital spending for commercially insured residents of states with all-payer global budgets grow more slowly than the national rate, without readmission rates rising?

**Sources:** [Pew Research, May 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2026/05/11/americans-see-health-care-costs-deficit-inflation-as-big-problems-facing-the-nation/), [Gallup, March 2026](https://news.gallup.com/poll/707732/healthcare-reclaims-top-spot-among-domestic-worries.aspx), [Peterson-KFF spending comparison](https://www.healthsystemtracker.org/chart-collection/health-spending-u-s-compare-countries/), [Peterson-KFF life expectancy](https://www.healthsystemtracker.org/chart-collection/u-s-life-expectancy-compare-countries/), [RAND hospital prices](https://www.rand.org/news/press/2024/05/13.html), [RAND drug prices](https://www.rand.org/news/press/2024/02/01.html), [Health Affairs administrative waste](https://www.healthaffairs.org/content/briefs/role-administrative-waste-excess-us-health-spending), [FTC PBM report](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/07/ftc-releases-interim-staff-report-prescription-drug-middlemen), [Peterson-KFF medical debt](https://www.healthsystemtracker.org/brief/the-burden-of-medical-debt-in-the-united-states/), [KFF 2026 marketplace](https://www.kff.org/affordable-care-act/what-we-know-so-far-about-2026-aca-marketplace-enrollment-premiums-and-deductibles/), [KFF 2025 reconciliation law](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/health-provisions-in-the-2025-federal-budget-reconciliation-law/), [Mathematica Maryland evaluation](https://www.mathematica.org/news/maryland-saved-medicare-more-than-780-million-using-new-approach-to-payment)

## PLANT 13 — Biosphere
### Prices Reset Higher and Stay There: How the 2021–23 Surge Became a Lasting Cost-of-Living Gap

Inflation is a rate of change. The cost of living is a level. From February 2020 to August 2026 the Consumer Price Index rose from 258.678 to 334.980, an increase of 29.5%. When inflation slows, prices keep rising, only more slowly. They fall back only during deflation, and deflation is rare. So how much strain a household feels depends on two things: whether its pay has caught up to the new price level, and whether the categories that rose fastest take up a large share of its budget. In April 2026, 66% of U.S. adults called inflation a "very big problem" (74% of Democrats and 55% of Republicans), up from 63% overall in February 2025.

**Prices vs. wages:** The result depends on the start date and on which price index is used. Pew analyzed Current Population Survey data from December 2020 to December 2025. Real median weekly earnings fell 3.2% measured against CPI-U and 1.0% measured against the PCE index. Over 2015–2025, wages outpaced inflation under every index.

**Which categories rose most (BLS index levels, Feb 2020 → Aug 2026):** Gasoline +64.7%. Energy overall +58.1%. Motor vehicle insurance +47.1%. Electricity +46.1%. Food away from home +37.3%. Rent of primary residence +32.6%. Food at home +31.7%. Core CPI (excluding food and energy) +26.5%. In the 12 months to August 2026, energy rose 16.3% and motor vehicle insurance fell 5.1%.

**What drove 2021–23 (three explanations that compete on weight, not on whether each played a part):** *Supply and price shocks:* Bernanke and Blanchard (2023) found that most of the surge came from "shocks to prices given wages": commodity spikes, and supply limits in sectors where demand shifted. Tight labor markets mattered less at first but had longer-lasting effects. *Fiscal stimulus:* the San Francisco Fed estimated that U.S. pandemic transfers "may have contributed to an increase in inflation of about 3 percentage points by the fourth quarter of 2021," while noting "considerable uncertainty." *Monetary policy:* the Fed held its target rate at 0–0.25% until March 2022, then raised it 4.25 points that year. A 2024 Fed staff note concluded that the Fed's own conditions for raising rates "locked the Committee into holding the policy rate at the zero lower bound longer than was optimal."

**Tariffs, 2025–26:** Yale's Budget Lab puts the average statutory tariff rate at 11.0% and estimates the eventual effect on consumer prices at about 0.7%, or roughly $1,100 per household per year.

**Proposed solution:** Belgium automatically adjusts wages to an index of prices. The IMF found this "has protected purchasing power," but also that it "places burden on employers, threatening competitiveness," and that its correction rules "can imply prolonged no or limited real wage increases post-shocks." The IMF recommended taking volatile commodities out of the index and replacing broad compensation with "targeted fiscal support to vulnerable groups." The trade-off: indexation keeps pay in line with prices, but it can also keep inflation going longer, which is the wage-price spiral risk.

**The testable question:** By December 2027, will cumulative growth in U.S. median weekly earnings since February 2020 (CPS method) equal or exceed the cumulative rise in core CPI over the same period (26.5% as of August 2026)?

## PLANT 14 — Biosphere
### Interest Now Matches Medicare: The Mechanics of a Debt Growing Faster Than the Economy

The deficit is one year's spending minus that year's revenue. The debt is the total of past borrowing. When debt grows faster than the economy and interest rates are above zero, interest becomes a line item that pushes itself higher: borrowing pays the interest, and the new borrowing adds more interest. In Pew's April 2026 survey, 64% of Americans called the federal deficit a "very big problem": 62% of Republicans and 66% of Democrats. Since May 2024, concern has risen 31 points among Democrats and fallen 9 points among Republicans.

**Current size:** Gross federal debt was $38.91 trillion as of May 5, 2026. The FY2025 deficit was $1.8 trillion: revenues were $5.235 trillion and outlays were $7.010 trillion. CBO projects a FY2026 deficit of $1.9 trillion (5.8% of GDP), against a 50-year average of 3.8%. CBO projects debt held by the public at 101% of GDP in 2026, rising to 120% by 2036. The previous record was 106%, set in 1946.

**Interest compared with other programs:** In FY2025, net interest was $1.028 trillion, the first time it passed $1 trillion. Medicare cost $992 billion and Defense Department military spending was $868 billion. CBO projects net interest at $1.039 trillion in 2026, which is 3.3% of GDP, the same share as Medicare, and $2.144 trillion (4.6% of GDP) by 2036. As of April 2026, the average interest rate on marketable federal debt was 3.373%.

**What drives the growth (the data each side relies on):** Those who point to spending cite outlays of 23.3% of GDP in 2026, against a 50-year average of 21.2%, and Social Security and Medicare growing from 8.5% to 10.1% of GDP by 2036 as the population ages. Revenue, at 17.5% of GDP in 2026, is near its 50-year average of 17.3%. Those who point to revenue cite CBO's estimate that the 2025 reconciliation act adds $4.7 trillion to deficits over 2026–2035. CBO credits tariffs with reducing deficits by $3.0 trillion; customs duties reached $195 billion in FY2025, up 153%. That estimate depends on tariff policy staying in place.

**Debt-ceiling history:** The debt limit is a cap on borrowing to pay for spending Congress has already approved. It has been changed more than 100 times since World War II, including eight suspensions since 2013. The July 2025 law raised it by $5 trillion, to $41.1 trillion, and it is expected to bind again in mid-to-late 2027. Government auditors estimated that the 2011 standoff raised borrowing costs by $1.3 billion in FY2011.

**Proposed solution:** Switzerland's "debt brake" is a fiscal rule written into its constitution. 85% of voters approved it in 2001, and it took effect in 2003. It caps federal spending at expected revenue, adjusted for the business cycle, so deficits are allowed in downturns and must be offset by surpluses in good years. Federal debt fell by about CHF 27 billion from 2003 to 2019, and the debt ratio is around 30% of GDP. An emergency clause allowed CHF 30 billion of COVID spending, to be repaid by 2035. The trade-offs: Switzerland started from a much lower debt level than the U.S.; the rule does not decide whether the gap is closed with taxes or with spending; escape clauses can weaken it; and a U.S. version would need a statute or a constitutional amendment.

**The testable question:** Across CBO's next three baseline projections, if no new laws are passed, does projected debt held by the public in 2036 stay at or above 120% of GDP? And does any enacted rule or policy change flatten that 10-year path or bring it down?

**Sources:** [Pew Research Center, May 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2026/05/11/americans-see-health-care-costs-deficit-inflation-as-big-problems-facing-the-nation/), [CBO, Budget and Economic Outlook 2026–2036](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61882), [CBO, Executive Summary PDF](https://www.cbo.gov/system/files/2026-02/61882-Executive-Summary.pdf), [CBO, Monthly Budget Review FY2025](https://www.cbo.gov/system/files/2025-11/61307-MBR-FY25-final.pdf), [Joint Economic Committee, debt as of May 2026](https://www.jec.senate.gov/public/index.cfm/republicans/2026/5/national-debt-reaches-38-91-trillion-increased-2-70-trillion-year-over-year-increased-10-75-trillion-in-five-years), [CRFB, Debt Ceiling Q&A](https://www.crfb.org/papers/qa-everything-you-should-know-about-debt-ceiling), [Swiss Federal Finance Administration, Debt brake](https://www.efd.admin.ch/en/the-debt-brake)

## PLANT 15 — Biosphere
### Supply, Rates and Rules: Why Homes Now Cost Five Years of Income

How affordable housing is comes down to a ratio: what homes cost against what households earn. That ratio is set by three things: how many homes exist relative to the number of households, the price of borrowing, and the local rules on what may be built. All three tightened at the same time. According to Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies, the median single-family home cost 3.2 times median household income through the 1990s, 4.1 times in 2019, and 5.0 times in 2024.

**Cost burdens:** In 2026, 22.7 million renter households were cost-burdened, meaning they paid more than 30% of income for housing. That is 49% of all renters and 2.3 million more than in 2019. Of these, 12.1 million (26% of renters) paid more than half their income. Home prices have risen 54% since 2020. Existing-home sales fell to 4.1 million, a three-decade low. Homeowners' insurance premiums rose 72% from 2019 to 2025.

**Rates and lock-in:** The 30-year fixed mortgage rate averaged 7.03% on September 24, 2026, up from 6.30% a year earlier. Most owners hold loans at lower rates, which makes them reluctant to sell. An FHFA study found that each percentage point by which market rates exceed an owner's rate cuts the probability of a sale by 18.1%. This lock-in prevented 1.33 million sales from mid-2022 to late 2023, and it raised prices by 5.7%, more than offsetting the 3.3% drop that higher rates alone would have caused.

**Shortage estimates (methods differ):** Freddie Mac 3.7 million (Q3 2024). Up for Growth 3.78 million (2023). Zillow 4.7 million (2023). Brookings 4.9 million (Q4 2023). National Association of Realtors 5.5 million (2020). The Congressional Research Service notes there is "not universal agreement" that a national shortage exists; some researchers see shortages only in certain places or housing types.

**Austin case:** Austin built 120,000 homes from 2015 to 2024, enlarging its housing stock by 30%, against 9% nationally. From 2021 to 2023, Austin permitted 957 apartments per 100,000 residents; San Antonio permitted 346. The city's HOME reforms of 2023–24 cut the minimum lot size from 5,750 to 1,800 square feet. Median rent fell from $1,546 in December 2021 to $1,296 in January 2026. Over 2023–24, rents in large buildings fell 7% and rents in older Class C buildings fell 11%. Critics make three points: the construction boom "was created and permitted under Austin's land development code as it existed prior to" HOME; demand cooled at the same time; and most new units target households earning 80–120% of median family income.

**Minneapolis comparison:** Minneapolis's 2040 plan allowed duplexes and triplexes on lots once limited to single homes, and the city ended all parking mandates in 2021. From 2017 to 2022, its housing stock grew 12% and rents rose 1%. In the rest of Minnesota, housing grew 4% and rents rose 14%. Yet 87% of the roughly 21,000 permitted units were in buildings of 20 or more units; only 1% were in 2–4 unit buildings.

**Proposed solution:** Two kinds of zoning change have a track record: allowing more homes per lot through smaller minimum lots and duplex or triplex permission (Austin, Minneapolis), and allowing apartments along transit corridors without case-by-case approval (Minneapolis). The trade-offs: the evidence so far points more to large apartment buildings than to small-lot infill; benefits may not reach the lowest-income renters; and opponents in Austin raised displacement concerns.

**The testable question:** From 2026 to 2029, will Austin's lots under 5,750 square feet produce measurable new units, as counted in city permit data? And will metros that permit more than 800 units per 100,000 residents show lower growth in inflation-adjusted median rent than metros that permit fewer than 400, after controlling for population growth?

**Sources:** [JCHS State of the Nation's Housing 2026](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/interactive-item/files/Harvard_JCHS_State_of_the_Nations_Housing_2026_Press_Release.pdf), [JCHS: price-to-income ratio](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/blog/home-prices-surge-five-times-median-income-nearing-historic-highs), [Freddie Mac PMMS, Sept 24, 2026](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/09/24/3368592/0/en/mortgage-rates-average-7-03.html), [FHFA WP 24-03: lock-in](https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403), [CRS: Estimates of a "Housing Shortage"](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IN12628.html), [Pew: Austin rents](https://www.pew.org/en/research-and-analysis/articles/2026/03/18/austins-surge-of-new-housing-construction-drove-down-rents), [Austin Free Press: Trickle down](https://austinfreepress.org/trickle-down/), [Pew: Minneapolis reforms](https://www.pew.org/en/research-and-analysis/articles/2024/01/04/minneapolis-land-use-reforms-offer-a-blueprint-for-housing-affordability)

## PLANT 16 — Biosphere
### Overdose Deaths Fell About 35% From Their Peak, Yet About 4 in 5 Adults With Opioid Use Disorder Get No Medication for It

In the Pew survey of April 2026, 55% of adults called drug addiction a "very big problem" (62% of Republicans, 48% of Democrats). Overdose deaths depend on three things: how potent and contaminated the drug supply is, how many people use it, and the chance that an overdose is reversed or the person is treated. Each factor has its own data and its own policy levers.

**Trend:** Deaths peaked in June 2023 at about 110,000 over 12 months. By August 2025 they had fallen to about 72,000, a drop of roughly 35%. Fentanyl-involved deaths were cut in half over that period, while deaths involving only stimulants stayed stable. CDC's latest provisional count is 67,798 deaths in the 12 months ending March 2026, 12.3% fewer than a year earlier.

**Supply chain:** Mexican criminal organizations buy precursor chemicals mainly from China and India and make fentanyl in labs in Mexico. The DEA names the Sinaloa Cartel and the Jalisco New Generation Cartel (CJNG) as the main traffickers. About 5 in 10 counterfeit pills contained 2 mg or more of fentanyl, an amount that can be lethal depending on tolerance. Fentanyl purity declined through 2024, which the DEA links to producers having trouble getting precursors. DEA seized 9,950 kg of fentanyl in 2024, 29% less than in 2023.

**Treatment gap:** In 2021, about 2.5 million adults had opioid use disorder (OUD), and only 22% received medication for it, such as buprenorphine or methadone. People who received treatment by telehealth were about 38 times more likely to get medication. Until January 2023, doctors needed a special federal license, the X-waiver, to prescribe buprenorphine. Removing it increased the number of prescribers but changed the number of patients treated very little.

**Approaches tried:** *Oregon Measure 110 (2021)* decriminalized possession of small amounts. A Portland State University study found little evidence that it caused the rise in deaths, which it attributes mainly to fentanyl and COVID. Oregon's December 2025 state audit found that the state "cannot determine" whether the roughly $800 million spent cut overdoses or improved access to care. The legislature re-criminalized possession through HB 4002 in 2024. *Portugal (decriminalized in 2001)* had by 2018 the lowest drug-related death rate in Western Europe, and HIV infections from injection drug use had fallen 90%. Treatment funding was later cut from $82.7 million to $17.4 million; since then, drug use prevalence rose to 12.8% and overdose rates reached a 12-year high. *Naloxone*, the drug that reverses opioid overdoses: a meta-analysis found 97.3% survival across 10,328 reported administrations.

**Proposed solution:** Make telehealth prescribing of buprenorphine permanent, building on the pandemic-era rules, and distribute naloxone directly in communities, where community-level studies show 25–46% lower overdose death rates. The trade-offs: the telehealth finding is an association, not proof of cause; over-the-counter naloxone, available since March 2023, has shown limited impact so far, with only 9% of pharmacies stocking it; and Portugal suggests that changing the law without sustained treatment funding may not keep its gains.

**The testable question:** By 2028, do the states with the largest increases in the share of OUD patients receiving medication also show larger declines in opioid overdose death rates than the states with the smallest increases?

## PLANT 17 — Biosphere
### Most U.S. Gun Deaths Are Suicides, Gun Homicides Cluster in Small Networks, and Policy Research Rates Few Measures as Well-Supported

Public debate treats "gun deaths" as one problem. The data show two problems that work differently. Suicides make up most gun deaths and are spread across the population. Homicides are fewer and are concentrated among small groups of people. Policies aimed at one group may do little for the other. Pew found in April 2026 that 49% of adults call gun violence a "very big problem" (27% of Republicans, 68% of Democrats).

**Deaths by category:** In 2024, the latest complete year of CDC data, 44,447 people died from gun-related injuries. Of these, 27,593 (62%) were suicides, 15,364 (35%) were homicides, 636 involved law enforcement, 450 were accidental and 404 were undetermined. Gun homicides fell 27% from a record 20,958 in 2021 to 15,364 in 2024. Gun suicides moved the other way and reached their highest count in 2024. The gun suicide rate was 7.6 per 100,000 people, close to the record of 7.7 set in 1977.

**Concentration:** A six-year Chicago study found that 70% of nonfatal gunshot victims were located in co-offending networks made up of less than 6% of the city's population. Each 1% increase in a person's exposure to gunshot victims in their immediate network raised their own odds of being shot by about 1.1%.

**What the policy evidence rates:** RAND's review, updated January 2026, included 207 studies. It found "supportive" evidence, its strongest rating, that child-access prevention (CAP) laws reduce youth suicides, unintentional child deaths, and youth homicides and assault injuries. It found supportive evidence that stand-your-ground laws and shall-issue concealed-carry laws increase homicides. It found moderate evidence that background checks reduce firearm and total homicides, and that waiting periods reduce firearm suicides and total homicides. Evidence on background checks and suicide, and on mass shootings, was inconclusive. RAND notes that weak evidence does not mean a policy is ineffective; it means the research is incomplete.

**Defensive gun use:** The CDC cites a range of 60,000 to 2.5 million defensive gun uses per year, depending on how studies ask the question. The National Crime Victimization Survey implies about 70,040 per year (2014–2018). Kleck and Gertz (1995) estimated 2.1 to 2.5 million. Kleck argues that gun owners underreport because they fear legal consequences. Hemenway argues that phone surveys inflate the count through social desirability bias and "telescoping," meaning respondents place events inside the survey window that happened before it.

**Proposed solution:** Child-access prevention laws, which hold adults liable when a minor gets hold of a negligently stored gun, are the measure with RAND's strongest rating on youth deaths. Studies found ratios of 0.75–0.90 for youth harm, and in one analysis child firearm deaths fell to 78% of the expected level; felony versions showed stronger effects. The trade-offs: much of the measured effect came from a single state, Florida; evidence for adults is limited; and gun-rights advocates say storage rules can slow access in an emergency. A second option is waiting periods, which have moderate evidence for reducing firearm suicides, the largest category of gun deaths.

**The testable question:** In states that enact a felony CAP law between 2026 and 2029, does the combined rate of youth (under 18) firearm suicide and unintentional death fall at least 10% more within four years than in comparable states without one, measured in CDC WONDER?

**Sources:** [Pew – What the data says about gun deaths](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/04/28/what-the-data-says-about-gun-deaths-in-the-us/), [Pew – National problems appendix, May 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2026/05/11/national-problems-appendix/), [RAND – What Science Tells Us About Gun Policies](https://www.rand.org/research/gun-policy/key-findings/what-science-tells-us-about-the-effects-of-gun-policies.html), [RAND – CAP laws and unintentional injuries](https://www.rand.org/research/gun-policy/analysis/child-access-prevention/unintentional-injuries.html), [Papachristos et al. – Tragic, but not Random](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps14-005), [CDC – About Firearm Injury and Death](https://www.cdc.gov/firearm-violence/about/index.html), [The Trace – How often are guns used for self-defense?](https://www.thetrace.org/2022/06/defensive-gun-use-data-good-guys-with-guns/)

## PLANT 18 — Biosphere
### Violent Crime Is Falling Fast, but Most Violent Crimes Still End Without an Arrest, and the Odds of Being Caught Deter More Than Sentence Length

Violent crime rose sharply in 2020 and has fallen since 2022 to at or below pre-pandemic levels. Public perception has trailed the data. A less visible variable sits underneath both: the clearance rate, meaning the share of reported crimes that end in an arrest or are otherwise resolved. Criminology finds that offenders respond more to the odds of being caught than to the size of the punishment. If that holds, clearance rates are a direct lever on crime. Pew found in April 2026 that 47% of adults call violent crime a "very big problem" (55% of Republicans, 38% of Democrats).

**Spike and decline:** Homicide rose a historic 30% from 2019 to 2020 (Council on Criminal Justice). Preliminary FBI data for 2025, covering agencies that serve 96% of the population, show murder down 18.1%, robbery down 18.5%, aggravated assault down 7.2% and violent crime overall down 9.3%. In a 36-city sample for the first half of 2026, homicide was 18% below the same period of 2025, 31% below 2019 and 51% below 2022. Carjacking fell 47% and robbery 17%. Not every category fell: domestic violence rose 8% and sexual assault rose 3%.

**Perception gap:** In Gallup's October 2025 poll, 49% of adults said there was more crime in the U.S. than a year earlier, down from 77% in 2023 and 64% in 2024, during the largest one-year drop in murder the FBI has recorded since 1937. By party, 54% of Republicans, 49% of independents and 46% of Democrats said crime had risen. Only 30% reported more crime where they live.

**Clearance rates:** In 2022, the most recent year with full figures in the Pew summary, 52.3% of murders were cleared, along with 41.4% of aggravated assaults, 26.1% of rapes and 23.2% of robberies. Overall, 36.7% of violent crimes were cleared. In 2013, the murder clearance rate was 64.1% and the aggravated assault rate was 57.7%. Victims also report only 41.5% of violent crimes to police, so the chance that a given offense ends in an arrest is lower than these rates suggest.

**Certainty versus severity:** The National Institute of Justice summarizes the research this way: "the certainty of being caught is a vastly more powerful deterrent than the punishment." It also says increasing sentence severity "does little to deter crime."

**Proposed solution:** Put resources into investigations. From 2012 to 2014, Boston police added staff, training, standard procedures and peer reviews to homicide investigations, working with the Suffolk County District Attorney. Homicide clearance rose from 47.1% (2007–2011) to 56.9%, better than trends in comparison jurisdictions. The trade-offs: detectives and analysts cost money; budgets are finite; and the gain came from a single city using a quasi-experimental design (a comparison against other places, not a randomized trial). The NIJ also identifies hot-spots policing as a way to raise the perceived odds of being caught; critics raise concerns about how intensely it concentrates police contact in particular neighborhoods.

**The testable question:** In cities that measurably expand investigator and analyst staffing for homicides and nonfatal shootings, does the violent-crime clearance rate rise by at least 10 percentage points within three years, and does the shooting rate then fall faster than in comparable cities, using FBI and local data?

**Sources:** [Pew – National problems appendix, May 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2026/05/11/national-problems-appendix/), [Council on Criminal Justice – Mid-Year 2026 Update](https://counciloncj.org/crime-trends-in-u-s-cities-mid-year-2026-update/), [FBI – Early look at 2025 crime data](https://www.fbi.gov/news/press-releases/fbi-releases-historic-early-look-at-annual-crime-data), [Gallup – Crime Seen as Less Serious for Second Straight Year](https://news.gallup.com/poll/697124/crime-seen-less-serious-second-straight-year.aspx), [Pew – What the data says about crime](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2024/04/24/what-the-data-says-about-crime-in-the-us/), [NIJ – Five Things About Deterrence](https://nij.ojp.gov/topics/articles/five-things-about-deterrence), [Harvard Kennedy School – BPD program raises homicide clearance](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/centers/taubman/programs-research/rappaport/news/northeastern-university-study-finds-BPD-Program-Raises-Homicide-Clearance-Rates)

## PLANT 19 — Biosphere
### Immigration Processing: Fewer People Arriving at the Border, but the Courts and Visa Queues Behind It Still Move Slowly

The immigration system runs as a set of linked queues. Enforcement decides how many people enter. The immigration courts decide who may stay. A fixed set of legal visa quotas decides who can come in lawfully, and how long they wait. When intake runs faster than decisions for years, backlogs build. Long waits then change how people behave and make the system harder to plan. Each side of the debate leans on a different part of this machinery. Pew found in April 2026 that 38% of adults call illegal immigration a "very big problem."

**Intake (border encounters):** Border Patrol encounters at the U.S.–Mexico border were 1,659,206 in FY2021, 2,206,436 in FY2022, 2,045,838 in FY2023 and 1,530,523 in FY2024. In FY2025 they were 237,538, the lowest of any fiscal year since 1970. Every month since February 2025 has been below 10,000. People who favor enforcement point to this drop as the measured result of the 2025 policy changes.

**Courts (stock, staff, outcomes):** Immigration courts held nearly 3.8 million pending cases as of Q3 2025 by the court system's (EOIR's) own count, about 2.4 million of them asylum cases; TRAC, which counts differently, reports about 3.09 million active cases as of August 2026. Earlier in 2025 the average wait across all cases was about 1.7 years; the estimated wait for an asylum case was 4.3 years (early-2024 estimate). The court had 735 judges at the end of FY2024. In FY2025–FY2026, 279 judges were fired or resigned, about 38% of that number, and the administration hired 192 new judges plus 53 temporary judges. Of the 743 judges deciding asylum cases in FY2026, only 43% were on the bench before January 2025. Asylum grants were above 50% early in the Biden term and 32% at its end. In June 2026, judges denied 94% of asylum cases and granted 771. One reading is stricter screening of weak claims. The other is that the outcome depends on which judges are deciding.

**Legal channels (quotas):** Congress set the employment-based green-card cap at 140,000 a year in 1990. No single country may receive more than about 7%, roughly 10,000 people. As of December 2025, 1.2 million people were in the employment-based backlog, nearly one million of them from India (about 79%). An Indian applicant in the EB-2 category filing in 2026 faces a projected wait of up to 179 years.

**Population and labor dependence:** Pew estimates the unauthorized population at a record 14 million in 2023, up 3.5 million in two years. These workers made up 5.6% of the U.S. workforce, including 15% in construction and 14% in agriculture. Pew says the population "has probably started to decline" in 2025.

**2025 law:** H.R. 1, signed July 4, 2025, added $170.7 billion for enforcement, including $45 billion for detention. It created a $100 asylum filing fee plus $100 for each year a case is pending, and it caps the number of immigration judges at 800 from November 1, 2028.

**Proposed solution:** Use time-limited dockets backed by matching judge capacity. The FY2022 Dedicated Docket brought average asylum completion down to 276 days, and asylum decisions doubled (51,607 against 24,810 the year before). The trade-off was measured too: grant rates for cases decided within 3–18 months fell from 43% to 31%, compared with 52–64% for slower cases, and applicants on the fast track were less likely to have a lawyer. Faster dockets need either more judges or a later judge cap, and they need legal counsel available, or speed comes at the cost of accuracy.

**The testable question:** By September 30, 2027, with about 740 judges and the 800-judge cap scheduled, will the immigration courts' pending caseload, by EOIR's official count, fall below 3.0 million, and will the average wait for a newly filed asylum case fall below 2 years?

## PLANT 20 — Biosphere
### AI and Jobs: Many Jobs Are Exposed, but the Measured Effect So Far Is Narrow and Concentrated in Entry-Level Work

"Exposure" means a share of a job's tasks that AI could do. It is not the same as displacement, where the job is lost. Whether exposure turns into job losses depends on three things: whether firms use AI to replace work or to support it, how fast productivity gains turn into new demand, and how the resulting income is split between wages and capital. Past automation waves were mixed: old tasks disappeared and new occupations appeared, but the gains did not reach all workers equally. Pew found in April 2026 that 36% of adults call unemployment a "very big problem."

**Estimated exposure:** The OpenAI/Penn study "GPTs are GPTs" estimates that about 80% of U.S. workers could have at least 10% of their tasks affected, and about 19% could have at least half affected. Higher-income jobs are more exposed than in earlier automation waves.

**What has been measured:** Stanford researchers used ADP payroll records covering millions of workers. They found that workers aged 22–25 in the most AI-exposed occupations had a 13% relative decline in employment since late 2022, while older workers in the same occupations held steady or grew. The losses were concentrated where AI automates tasks rather than supporting workers. The Yale Budget Lab analysis (updated September 15, 2026) finds that the occupational mix "is not yet changing in ways that clearly align" with AI, and that AI-usage measures show "no connection" to unemployment. Both results can be true at once: a narrow effect on young workers inside an aggregate that has not changed.

**Productivity and income split:** Nonfarm productivity rose 2.2% from 2024 to 2025, and at a 2.8% annual rate in Q4 2025. Labor's share of output, meaning the part paid out as worker compensation, was 53.8% in Q4 2025, the lowest point in a series that starts in 1947.

**Proposed solution:** Two tested approaches. *Wage insurance* pays displaced workers up to half the gap between their old and new wage for up to two years. Under Trade Adjustment Assistance, where it applied only to workers 50 and older, it raised employment by 8–17 points in the two years after a layoff and raised earnings by over $18,000 (26%) across four years; extra tax receipts and lower unemployment payments fully covered its cost. *Cash floors* have mixed results. The OpenResearch study gave 1,000 people $1,000 a month for three years, compared with 2,000 people receiving $50 a month, and found 2.0 points lower labor participation, 1.3–1.4 fewer hours worked per week and about $1,500 a year less earned income. Alaska's universal annual dividend showed no effect on employment and a 1.8-point (17%) increase in part-time work. The trade-off: wage insurance supports people going back to work but helps only those who find new jobs; cash floors cover everyone but cost more and somewhat reduce hours worked.

**The testable question:** Between September 2025 and September 2027, will the employment gap between workers aged 22–25 and older workers in the most AI-exposed occupations widen beyond the 13% measured by Stanford, while the Yale Budget Lab still finds no aggregate AI footprint?

**Sources:** [GPTs are GPTs (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.10130v3), [Stanford SIEPR – Canaries in the Coal Mine](https://siepr.stanford.edu/publications/working-paper/canaries-coal-mine-six-facts-about-recent-employment-effects-artificial), [Yale Budget Lab – Tracking the Impact of AI](https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/tracking-impact-ai-labor-market), [BLS – Productivity and Costs Q4 2025](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/prod2_03052026.htm), [OpenResearch – NBER employment findings](https://www.openresearchlab.org/findings/nber-working-paper-employment), [NBER – Alaska Permanent Fund labor effects](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24312), [VoxEU/CEPR – Wage insurance for displaced workers](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/wage-insurance-trade-displaced-workers-middle-ground-alternative-rising-protectionism)
